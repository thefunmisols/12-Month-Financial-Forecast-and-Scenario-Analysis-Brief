# 12-Month Financial Forecast, Scenario Analysis & Executive Decision Support

## Executive Summary

Financial forecasting is not simply about predicting future revenue.

A strong forecast should help management understand:

**Where the business is heading, what assumptions are driving performance, what could go wrong, how sensitive profitability is to changing conditions, and what actions leadership should take.**

In this project, I developed a **12-month financial forecast and scenario analysis** designed to support management planning and financial decision-making.

The project covered:

* Base Case financial forecasting
* Revenue growth modelling
* Cost and profitability forecasting
* Optimistic and Downside scenario modelling
* Variance analysis
* Financial sensitivity analysis
* Risk identification
* KPI interpretation
* Executive recommendation writing

The objective was to transform forecast assumptions into **clear financial insights that management could use for planning, budgeting, cost control, and strategic decision-making**.

---

# Project Objective

The core objective of this project was to answer several important management questions:

> **What could the company's financial performance look like over the next 12 months?**

> **How would profitability change if business conditions are better or worse than expected?**

> **Which assumptions have the greatest impact on financial performance?**

> **What actions should management take to protect profitability if growth underperforms?**

Rather than relying on a single forecast, I created multiple scenarios to show management the **range of possible financial outcomes**.

# Step 1 — Built the 12-Month Base Case Forecast

I started by developing a **Base Case forecast**, representing the most probable financial outcome based on the assumptions provided.

<img width="1357" height="645" alt="12-Month Base Case Financial Forecast" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/779e70e8-29e1-42b0-b6e4-2cb154f22e8e" />

The Base Case started with:

* **Month 1 Revenue:** $500,000
* **Monthly Revenue Growth:** 5%
* **Cost of Services:** 30% of Revenue
* **Marketing:** 10% of Revenue
* **Operating Expenses Months 1–6:** $100,000 monthly
* **Operating Expenses Months 7–12:** $120,000 monthly

Revenue was projected using:

**Next Month Revenue = Previous Month Revenue × (1 + Growth Rate)**

I then calculated:

**Cost of Services = Revenue × Cost of Services %**

**Marketing = Revenue × Marketing %**

**Net Operating Profit = Revenue − Cost of Services − Marketing − Operating Expenses**

The Base Case acts as the financial benchmark against which alternative outcomes can be measured.

Management can use it to support:

* Budget preparation
* Resource allocation
* Headcount planning
* Expense management
* Performance targets
* Cash-flow discussions
* Investment decisions
* Business valuation
* Strategic planning

Most importantly, the Base Case creates a structured financial expectation against which **actual performance can later be measured**.

# Step 3 — Scenario Analysis

A single forecast can create false confidence.

Business conditions change.

Revenue may outperform expectations. Costs may rise. Marketing may become less efficient. Operating expenses may increase faster than expected.

To capture this uncertainty, I created three scenarios:

### Base Case

Represents the most likely financial outcome.

### Optimistic Case

Represents stronger-than-expected revenue growth and improved cost efficiency.

### Downside Case

Represents slower growth and greater cost pressure.

# Key Management Insight

The analysis shows that profitability is particularly sensitive to:

* Revenue growth
* Cost of Services
* Marketing efficiency
* Operating expense growth

The difference between the Optimistic and Downside outcomes demonstrates why management should not rely on a single-point forecast.

Scenario planning helps decision-makers understand:

> **What happens if our assumptions are wrong?**

This is essential for effective budgeting, FP&A, cash planning, investment decisions, and risk management.

# Step 5 — Variance & Driver Analysis

I also assessed how forecast performance should be compared with actual business performance.

The Month 1 Base Case forecasts:

* **Revenue:** $500,000
* **Cost of Services:** $150,000
* **Marketing:** $50,000
* **Operating Expenses:** $100,000
* **Net Operating Profit:** $200,000

However, identifying a variance is not enough.

A finance professional must understand the **driver behind the variance**.

For example, a revenue variance could result from:

* Price changes
* Sales volume
* Product mix
* Customer mix
* Customer acquisition
* Conversion rates
* Market conditions

The model assumes 5% monthly revenue growth, so management should validate this assumption against actual operational data rather than accepting the forecast automatically.

---

# Understanding Cost Behaviour

An important aspect of the model is distinguishing between **variable and relatively fixed expenses**.

Cost of Services and Marketing are modelled as percentages of revenue.

This means:

**Higher Revenue → Higher Variable Costs**

However, Operating Expenses remain fixed within each six-month period.

As a result:

**Revenue growth has a disproportionately positive effect on operating profit once fixed costs have been covered.**

This is a critical consideration in financial planning because understanding cost behaviour helps management predict how changes in sales will affect profitability.

---

# Sensitivity Analysis

I also examined how changes in key assumptions could affect profitability.

With Cost of Services at 30% of revenue and Marketing at 10%, combined variable costs equal:

**40% of Revenue**

Therefore, each additional **$1 of revenue contributes approximately $0.60 toward fixed costs and operating profit**, assuming the cost ratios remain unchanged.

This creates an important management insight:

> **Small changes in revenue growth or cost percentages can have a material impact on operating profit.**

For example:

* A **10% annual revenue shortfall** could reduce operating profit by approximately **$477,514**.
* A **1 percentage-point increase in Cost of Services** could reduce annual profit by approximately **$79,586**.

This demonstrates why management should continuously monitor the assumptions driving the forecast.

---

# Executive Recommendation to the Finance Manager

After completing the financial analysis, I prepared an executive recommendation translating the model into a clear management decision.

The Base Case indicates strong revenue and profitability growth.

However, the forecast is particularly dependent on maintaining the assumed **5% monthly compound revenue growth rate**.

If revenue growth consistently underperforms, the effect compounds throughout the forecast period and reduces both annual revenue and the contribution available to cover operating expenses.

Other important risks include:

* Service delivery cost inflation
* Marketing expenditure without sufficient revenue generation
* Increased operating expenses
* Weak customer collections
* Working-capital pressure
* Liquidity constraints

The forecast also highlights an important distinction:

> **Accounting profit is not the same as cash availability.**

Because the model does not explicitly incorporate collection timing or working-capital requirements, management should avoid interpreting projected operating profit as immediately available cash.

---

# Strategic Recommendation

The model assumes that monthly operating expenses increase from **$100,000 to $120,000 beginning in Month 7**.

Rather than allowing this increase automatically, my recommendation was to make the additional expenditure **conditional on a formal Month 6 performance review**.

Approval should depend on:

* Demonstrated revenue growth
* Maintained contribution margins
* Marketing efficiency
* Cash availability
* Business performance against forecast

This introduces **financial discipline into expansion spending**.

If performance supports the investment, management can proceed.

If growth underperforms, management can delay or reduce the additional cost commitment to protect profitability and liquidity.

# Key Lessons From the Project

One of the strongest lessons from this project is:

> **A forecast is not valuable because it predicts the future perfectly. It is valuable because it helps management understand the financial consequences of different possible futures.**

Good forecasting therefore requires more than calculations.

It requires understanding:

**Assumptions → Business Drivers → Financial Outcomes → Risks → Management Actions**

A strong financial analyst should be able to answer:

* What assumptions are driving this forecast?
* What happens if revenue misses expectations?
* Which expenses scale with revenue?
* Which costs are fixed?
* What variables have the greatest impact on profitability?
* What is the downside scenario?
* What should management monitor?
* When should management change course?
* What strategic action does the analysis support?

That is the approach I applied throughout this project.
